The Cloud Has a Body

The AI industry draws itself as five campuses and a weightless cloud. This map follows the bill. Decisions and profits sit at a handful of addresses in California and Washington; the mines, the water, the electricity and the people who label and clean the data sit almost everywhere else.

Why most mines have no line

A line connects a place to one of the five headquarters only when the sources on that pin name the company. Data centres and labelling offices usually do: a contract, a permit, a payslip. The mines almost never do. Cobalt, lithium and rare earths pass through refineries, cathode makers and chip packagers, mostly in China, before any of the five companies touch them. That gap in the data is not an accident; it is what lets a company say it does not know where its materials come from.

What is missing from this map
  • Undersea cables and chip fabs. No fibre routes, no TSMC in Taiwan, no ASML in the Netherlands. The map would need two more layers and another week.
  • The end of the chain. E-waste sites like Agbogbloshie in Accra, where servers go to die, are not here.
  • People with no address. Crowd labellers work from kitchens and phones. Venezuela and Nigeria get one pin each for a whole country.
  • Exact coordinates. Pins sit at the published centre of a town, salt flat or mining district, not at a gate. Companies do not publish facility locations; some fought to keep them secret.
  • Names. Most moderators and labellers are under NDAs. The sources that quote them use first names or none.
  • Water and power numbers for most sites. The Dalles' figures took a 13-month public-records fight. Querétaro's officials declined to say. Most sites never had to say at all.
  • The projection. Web Mercator makes Iceland look larger than it is and the Congo smaller. The map's shape has a point of view before any pin is placed.
