## Run 1 – Open prompt, no themes suggested

**Prompt:** "Here is the full text of a poem. Please give me a literary analysis of it." (followed by the text file, which begins with the title and author line; the prompt itself did not name the poem or suggest any themes)

### Overview

Edgar Allan Poe's "The Raven" explores how grief can turn the desire for comfort into a form of self-torment. Its speaker, mourning the death of Lenore, encounters a raven whose repeated word—"Nevermore"—gradually comes to represent the loss of every hope he still possesses. Although the poem presents a potentially supernatural encounter, it also supports a psychological reading: the speaker gives the bird's apparently mechanical utterance increasingly devastating meanings.

**The poem's central tragedy is not simply that the speaker has lost Lenore, but that his search for certainty traps him within that loss.**

### Grief and the desire for relief

The opening establishes a speaker already emotionally and physically exhausted: he is "weak and weary," reading "forgotten lore" late at night. His books are an attempted distraction, but he admits that he has "vainly" sought "surcease of sorrow." Intellectual activity cannot relieve his grief.

The setting reflects this emotional condition. Midnight and "bleak December" suggest darkness, endings, and a world without renewal. The fire's "dying ember" produces a "ghost upon the floor," making even ordinary objects seem haunted. Before the raven appears, the speaker is already inhabiting an environment shaped by death and memory.

Lenore herself remains largely undefined. The speaker calls her "rare and radiant" and "sainted," but gives little sense of her individual personality. These idealizing descriptions make her the focus of his longing: she represents not just a lost beloved, but a happiness he believes he can never recover.

### The changing meaning of "Nevermore"

The raven's refrain is the poem's organizing device. Its wording never changes, but its significance changes with the speaker's questions.

At first, "Nevermore" seems almost comic: the speaker treats it as the bird's unusual name. Later, he offers a plausible explanation that it is the raven's "only stock and store," learned from an unhappy former master. This explanation suggests that the bird may not understand its own speech.

Nevertheless, the speaker begins treating the word as prophecy. He asks whether he can forget Lenore, whether there is relief for his suffering, and whether he will meet her again in heaven. Each repetition now appears to deny a deeper hope.

This creates an important tension: **the speaker recognizes that the raven may know only one word, yet asks questions whose predictable answer will hurt him.** His grief drives him to seek answers while also shaping those answers into absolute declarations of despair.

The early refrain, "nothing more," helps prepare this development. Initially, the speaker uses it to limit his fear: the sound is merely a visitor or the wind. By the end, "Nevermore" no longer limits the meaning of an event; it seems to foreclose every possible future.

### Symbolism: the raven and the bust of Pallas

The raven's black appearance and associations with the "Night's Plutonian shore" connect it with darkness and death. Yet its symbolic power depends less on what it does than on what the speaker believes it means. It remains largely motionless while his interpretation shifts from amusement to terror.

Its position on the **bust of Pallas** is especially significant. Pallas evokes Athena, associated with wisdom and reason. The raven perched above her image can therefore suggest grief and dread overshadowing rational thought. The speaker's learning offers no protection against his increasingly compulsive interpretations.

The chamber also functions as a representation of his inward life. He begins enclosed within sorrow, briefly opens doors and shutters to investigate the outside world, and ends with the raven permanently installed inside. What initially appears to be an external disturbance becomes an inescapable presence within his consciousness.

### Sound, rhythm, and repetition

The poem's musicality makes the speaker's obsession audible. Internal rhymes such as "dreary" and "weary," or "napping" and "tapping," create echoes within individual lines. Alliteration—especially in "Doubting, dreaming dreams"—intensifies the sense of thought circling back upon itself.

The predominantly trochaic rhythm, with stressed syllables followed by unstressed syllables, gives many lines an insistent, falling movement. The long lines accumulate sound and detail, while the shorter final lines of the stanzas deliver abrupt conclusions.

Repeated words and phrases enact the speaker's fixation:

> "Respite—respite"
> "tell me—tell me"
> "still is sitting, still is sitting"

As his agitation increases, repetition becomes less reassuring and more desperate. Meanwhile, the recurring "-ore" rhyme binds "door," "lore," "Lenore," and "Nevermore" together, making the poem's sound continually return to the beloved's name and the denial of her return.

### Religious allusions and supernatural uncertainty

The speaker's questions become increasingly spiritual. "Nepenthe" represents the possibility of forgetting sorrow; "balm in Gilead" invokes healing; and "Aidenn," evoking Eden or paradise, represents hope for reunion after death.

These references trace an escalation in what he seeks: first relief from memory, then healing, and finally assurance that death has not separated him from Lenore forever. "Nevermore" seems to reject all three.

However, the poem does not establish that the raven possesses supernatural knowledge. The speaker alternately calls it a bird, a prophet, a devil, and a fiend. His perceptions also become uncertain: "methought" qualifies the appearance of angelic presences, and the raven's eyes have only "the seeming" of a demon's. The supernatural interpretation remains possible, but the language leaves room for a mind overwhelmed by grief.

### The ending

The final stanza shifts from recounting events to describing an apparently continuing condition:

> "And the Raven, never flitting, still is sitting, still is sitting"

The encounter has become permanent in the speaker's account. The raven's shadow falls across the floor, and his soul seems trapped beneath it. This image transforms the bird into a figure for sorrow that dominates his entire existence.

Crucially, the speaker himself delivers the final "nevermore." The raven's word has entered his own voice. What began as an unexplained sound from outside has become his declaration about his future.

Ultimately, "The Raven" portrays grief becoming a closed cycle: the speaker searches for reassurance, interprets every answer through his loss, and arrives at the conviction that escape is impossible. The poem's unresolved question is whether the raven reveals his fate—or whether his despair makes its meaningless repetition sound like fate.

## Run 2 – The identical prompt, sent again in a fresh session

**Prompt:** "Here is the full text of a poem. Please give me a literary analysis of it." (word for word the same as Run 1, with the poem)

Edgar Allan Poe's "The Raven" explores how grief can turn the desire for reassurance into a cycle of self-torment. Its central drama is not simply the arrival of a mysterious bird, but the narrator's growing willingness to treat the bird's repeated word—"Nevermore"—as an authoritative verdict on his future. Through musical repetition, Gothic imagery, and an increasingly distressed first-person voice, the poem shows sorrow hardening into despair.

### Grief and the search for consolation

The narrator begins in a state of physical exhaustion and emotional vulnerability: "weak and weary," he reads old books in an attempt to find "surcease of sorrow" for the lost Lenore. Reading offers distraction but cannot resolve his loss. His wish for the morning suggests a desire to escape both the literal darkness and the painful thoughts that fill it.

Lenore remains largely undefined. The narrator describes her as "rare and radiant" and imagines her among angels, but offers no concrete account of her personality or their relationship. This idealization makes her seem less like a fully represented character than the center of his longing. Her absence shapes everything he sees: even a cushion becomes painful because he realizes that she will never sit on it again.

### The raven and the narrator's self-torment

The raven's significance changes as the narrator's emotions intensify. At first, its solemn behavior amuses him, "beguiling my sad fancy into smiling." Later, he calls it a "Prophet," a "thing of evil," and possibly a "devil." The bird itself changes very little; the narrator's interpretation changes dramatically.

Crucially, he initially supplies a reasonable explanation for its speech: "Nevermore" may be a phrase learned from an unhappy former owner. He therefore recognizes that the word need not carry any supernatural meaning. Nevertheless, he begins asking questions that make the predictable answer increasingly devastating: whether he can forget Lenore, whether healing exists, and whether they will meet in heaven.

**The narrator is both the sufferer and a participant in his own suffering.** Knowing the bird's limited vocabulary, he still frames questions that turn its answer into a denial of everything he hopes for. The poem leaves open whether the raven is supernatural, an ordinary bird, or chiefly a symbol of the narrator's grief. Its psychological force does not depend on resolving that uncertainty.

### Repetition, rhythm, and rhyme

The poem's sound patterns enact the narrator's obsessive thinking. Internal rhymes such as "dreary" and "weary," or "napping" and "tapping," create a strongly patterned musical texture. The predominantly trochaic rhythm—stressed syllables followed by unstressed ones—gives many lines an insistent, falling movement.

Repetition begins before the raven speaks. The narrator repeatedly reassures himself that the noise is "nothing more," trying to contain his fear through language. Once "Nevermore" enters the poem, this reassuring refrain gives way to one of absolute negation.

Although the bird repeats the same word, its meaning changes with each context. It first seems like a peculiar name, then an assurance that the bird will remain, and finally a denial of relief and reunion. The recurring "-ore" rhyme also binds "Lenore" to "Nevermore," making the beloved's name audibly inseparable from permanent loss.

### Gothic setting and symbolism

The setting makes the narrator's emotional condition visible. Midnight and "bleak December" suggest darkness, endings, and lifelessness. The "dying ember" that casts a "ghost upon the floor" introduces spectral imagery before the raven arrives, suggesting that the room is already haunted by memory.

The raven's perch on a bust of Pallas, associated with wisdom, is especially suggestive. A dark, seemingly irrational presence sits above an emblem of reason. This arrangement can symbolize grief overshadowing the narrator's intellectual attempts to understand or master it.

Religious and mythological references expand his private sorrow into questions about ultimate consolation. "Nepenthe" represents forgetfulness; the "balm in Gilead" evokes healing; and "Aidenn" points toward Eden or heavenly reunion. The repeated answer appears to close off each possibility, though these denials come through the narrator's interpretation of the bird rather than an independently established truth.

### The ending

In the final stanza, the encounter becomes an apparently permanent condition: the raven "still is sitting," and the narrator declares that his soul will never rise from its shadow. The shift to the present tense makes the suffering feel ongoing rather than safely confined to a remembered night.

The shadow brings together the physical room and the narrator's inner life. What begins as lamplight cast around a bird becomes an image of spiritual imprisonment. Ultimately, "The Raven" presents not just grief over death, but the frightening process by which a grieving mind turns uncertainty into certainty—and certainty into hopelessness.

## Run 3 – Historical context

**Prompt:** "Here is the full text of 'The Raven' by Edgar Allan Poe. What is the historical context of this poem, and how does that context shape how we should read it?" (with the poem)

Edgar Allan Poe's **"The Raven," published in 1845, belongs to the American Romantic period**, particularly its darker, Gothic tradition. Its historical context helps us read it not simply as a supernatural story, but as a carefully constructed exploration of grief, uncertain belief, and the mind's ability to turn ordinary events into sources of terror.

### Romanticism and the Gothic

Romantic literature often emphasized imagination, powerful emotion, and experiences beyond the reach of reason. Gothic writing explored those interests through darkness, isolation, death, and apparently supernatural encounters. "The Raven" brings these elements into a private chamber: midnight, "bleak December," dying embers, mysterious noises, and a grieving speaker.

But Poe never conclusively establishes that the raven is supernatural. The speaker himself suggests that "Nevermore" is a phrase the bird learned from a previous owner. **The central horror may therefore lie less in the bird than in the speaker's interpretation of it.** He moves from offering reasonable explanations to treating the raven as a prophet, choosing increasingly painful questions whose answer he already knows.

The bust of Pallas—Athena, associated with wisdom—sharpens this tension. The raven perched above it can suggest that fear and grief have gained ascendancy over rational understanding.

### Death, mourning, and religious consolation

Nineteenth-century readers lived with frequent deaths from illnesses that medicine could not effectively treat. Mourning and hopes of reunion after death were prominent concerns in religious life and literature.

The speaker's questions emerge from that context. "Balm in Gilead," a biblical reference, asks whether spiritual healing is possible. "Aidenn," a poetic form of Eden, invokes the possibility of reunion with Lenore in paradise. The raven's answer seems to deny both consolation and reunion.

However, **the poem does not establish that there is no afterlife**. It shows a distressed speaker accepting a bird's repeated word as an answer to questions the bird may not understand. This distinction lets us read the poem as a crisis of faith and interpretation rather than a straightforward statement of Poe's religious beliefs.

### Poe's personal experience of loss

Poe's life was marked by bereavement, including the early death of his mother. When "The Raven" appeared, his wife, Virginia, was living with tuberculosis; she died in 1847, two years after its publication.

Those circumstances make the poem's anxiety about losing a beloved person especially resonant. But Lenore should not simply be identified with Virginia, nor should the speaker be treated as Poe himself. Biography provides context, not a definitive explanation of a fictional work.

The poem also participates in a literary convention that idealized the dead beloved woman. Lenore appears as "rare," "radiant," and "sainted," but receives no voice or developed personal history. A critical reading can ask how this idealization makes her the center of the speaker's sorrow while leaving her own identity largely absent.

### Print culture and deliberate craftsmanship

"The Raven" circulated through newspapers and magazines and made Poe widely famous. Its strong rhythm, internal rhymes, alliteration, and memorable refrain make it particularly suited to recitation and repetition.

In his 1846 essay "The Philosophy of Composition," Poe presented the poem as the result of deliberate artistic planning. That retrospective account need not be accepted as a literal record of his writing process, but it encourages attention to technique: "Nevermore" stays the same while the speaker's questions make it progressively more devastating.

**Ultimately, historical context helps us read "The Raven" as both a Gothic performance and a psychological drama.** Its terror comes from uncertainty: an ordinary bird may become a supernatural messenger because a grieving person cannot stop making its single word mean the worst.

## Run 4 – Symbolism, literal vs. figurative

**Prompt:** "Here is the full text of 'The Raven' by Edgar Allan Poe. What does the raven symbolize in this poem? Should the bird be read literally or figuratively?" (with the poem)

The raven symbolizes **the narrator's persistent grief over Lenore, the finality of death, and his fear that he will never find relief**. It is best read both literally and figuratively: it can be an actual bird within the story while also embodying the narrator's despair.

### What does the raven symbolize?

The repeated word **"Nevermore"** gives the raven its symbolic power. At first, the word seems almost meaningless; the narrator even explains that it may be the bird's "only stock and store," learned from a former owner. As he asks increasingly painful questions, however, the same word becomes a denial of everything he hopes for: forgetting Lenore, healing his sorrow, and reuniting with her after death.

Importantly, **the narrator gives the bird's response these meanings**. Although he recognizes that it may know only one word, he continues asking questions whose predictable answer will torment him. The raven therefore represents not just loss but the narrator's inability to stop dwelling on it.

The ending strengthens this interpretation. The raven remains above the door, and the narrator declares that his soul will never rise from its shadow. That shadow suggests grief that has come to dominate his whole life.

### Literal or figurative?

These readings do not exclude each other. Literally, a bird enters through the window, perches, and repeats a learned word. Figuratively, its unchanging presence becomes an image of seemingly inescapable sorrow.

The narrator calls it a "prophet," "thing of evil," and possibly a "devil," but those descriptions do not establish that it really is supernatural. They reveal how his perception changes as his distress intensifies.

**The strongest reading is that a possibly ordinary bird becomes an extraordinary symbol through the narrator's grief.** Its "Nevermore" expresses his fear of permanent loss—not necessarily an objective truth about his future or the afterlife.
