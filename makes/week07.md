# Make 6 – The Cloud Has a Body: A Counter-Map of Where AI Is Mined, Powered and Cleaned

Every AI company publishes a map of itself: a campus, a logo, a cloud. I wanted the other map, the one that shows where the water bill goes. So I put 35 places on a world map and gave each one a number, a person and a source.

## Research Documentation

I took three of the four layers from the assignment and kept the fourth as a reference: mines and salt flats (cobalt in Congo; lithium in Chile, Bolivia and Argentina; rare earths in Inner Mongolia and Kachin State), data centres (eight in the United States, seven elsewhere) and the offices and platforms where people label data and moderate content (nine places, from Manila to Caracas). The five headquarters are there too, drawn small on purpose. That makes 35 places in 22 countries and 70 sources.

For every place I wanted two things: a number from an official or investigative source, and a person. The numbers come from the USGS, Ireland's statistics office, Virginia's legislative auditors, Global Witness, Amnesty and Epoch AI; the people come from AP, Reuters, TIME, Rest of World, the Bureau of Investigative Journalism, Mongabay, the BBC and others. Each pin shows what is here, who is here, one number, how the place connects to AI, and the links. Coordinates are Wikipedia's for the town or salt flat; companies do not publish gates, and I did not want to fake precision.

Two honesty problems came up. First, the mining sources are not about AI. They are about phones, electric cars and wind turbines. Cobalt, lithium and rare earths do end up in server batteries, backup power and hard-drive magnets, but no source traces a specific mine to a specific model. So every mining pin is labelled a "hardware link," and the counter-map draws a money line only where a source names one of the five companies: a contract, a permit, a payslip. Most mines have no line; the map explains why. Kate Crawford makes the same connection in *Atlas of AI*, where AI "is born from salt lakes in Bolivia and mines in Congo." I am following her argument, and the map says so.

Second, several of the most important numbers exist only because someone fought for them. The Dalles spent 13 months in court trying to keep Google's water use secret from *The Oregonian* before it settled and handed over the records. Google's Uruguay water figure came out in a court case. Querétaro officials declined to say how much water the state's data centres use. Ireland's statistics office cannot see data centres as a category and finds them by searching operator names. The Stargate coverage names executives and chip counts and not one resident of Abilene. Those gaps are listed on the map under "What is missing" rather than smoothed over.

I built the map with Leaflet and OpenFreeMap tiles instead of Google My Maps, partly because a Google map of Google's water use felt wrong and partly because I wanted to control what is big and what is small. The industry view makes the headquarters large and hides the mines and the labour; the counter-map does the opposite, from the same 35 rows. Halfway through, the free basemap I started with began demanding an API key and every tile turned into a watermark, so I switched providers. Even the blank background belongs to someone.

## Artist Statement

The pattern is a funnel. Decisions and profits sit at five addresses, four of them within 40 miles of each other. The rest spreads outward: data centres where power is cheap and bargaining power is weak (a cornfield in Indiana, a Black neighbourhood in Memphis); labelling and moderation in English-speaking former colonies; mines across the Global South. The money lines all point one way.

Who benefits: the companies, their investors, and people like me, who get an answer in two seconds. Who pays: Boxtown's lungs, The Dalles' water bills, the 140-plus Nairobi moderators diagnosed with PTSD, depression or anxiety, a family in Catamarca that went from 300 llamas to eight. In the counter-map the headquarters are tiny, the salt flats are big, and dashed lines run from each site to the company holding the contract. The industry view is the same data drawn the other way, and nothing on it is false. That is D'Ignazio and Klein's point about examining power: what you make big is the argument.

What is missing is most of it: undersea cables, chip fabs, e-waste, labelers with no address, moderators' names under NDAs, water numbers nobody was forced to release. Mimi Onuoha's point about missing datasets is that the gaps show what counts, and here they track power exactly: the more a place is paid, the more we know about it.

A tech CEO would draw my industry view and call it complete. A miner at Kamilombe would draw the shaft, the price of a sack and the buyer at the gate; the laptop never comes back to Kolwezi, so it would not be on his map. Neither map is neutral. In 1971 the Detroit Geographic Expedition and Institute mapped *Where Commuters Run Over Black Children* by asking the people who lived on the street. This map is my attempt to draw from that end.
